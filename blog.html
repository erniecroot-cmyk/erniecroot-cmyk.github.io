An optimistic look at the near-future of math.

In case the last post wasn't clear, math (the profession) is not going to end. It will just change form. There are a few things to consider and also some general comments:

1. We will still need people to learn advanced math, including how to do research. It is dangerous to society to become deskilled at doing math. For one thing, becoming deskilled means we would be ceding power to AI and a small number of organizations that control it.

2. People talk about how AI can teach math better than humans. That is true in certain narrow ways, but one could just as well have said that MOOCs could replace learning math at a university; but clearly they didn't, because you don't see a large number of humans doing research math just based on what they learned in a MOOC. If one has a great instructor (and I am not saying I am great), one can learn things by sitting in a classroom that one cannot learn by reading a book. I have my theories about why this is, and perhaps I will write this up in a future post. Just to mention one example, a professor may get really excited about something -- such as that the gcd of two integers can be written as an integer linear combination of those integers -- that the textbook may not play that up very much at all. What the professor knows, that you maybe don't get from just reading the textbook, is why that is such an important thing. Learning in class (as opposed to just out of a book) is like learning with emojis, or learning things with probabilities for measures of importance attached. (I can see the lack of this affecting my own knowlede in subjects like medicine. Despite having read a lot about "supplements" and "medicines" over the years, and having seen many lectures online, I am very far from any kind of "expert". If I had actually studied medicine in school, in person, it might be a different story.)

An Addendum added after writing this: this reminds me of the Elizabeth Holmes film You Can See Everything. In that film you can indeed see Holmes's last days before going to prison; however, just because you can see it doesn't mean you know what you're seeing. Likewise, just becasue you see everything written down in a math textbook doesn't mean you know what's important. It helps to have a guide!

3. Even if AI models can solve the greatest problems, proving many of the great conjectures of math, one can still produce other proofs, better and more personalized ones that plug into one's particular style and vision. For example, Paul Erdos once talked about finding the "book proof" for various conjectures. He playfully suggested the "supreme fascist" -- his way of saying "god" -- had a book containing the most elegant, short proofs of all the theorems of math, and that we should strive to find these for any given problem. If someone came up to him with a messy proof for something, he would politely acknowledge their achievement, but then would say, "now find the book proof". AI models can find book proofs some of the time; for example, OpenAI's standalone proof of the "no Siegel zeros" theorem would certainly qualify as one. But even then one could maybe try to find other proofs just as elegant and beautiful, but more to one's liking. (e.g. as book-proof-ish as the no-Siegel-zeros proof is, it is a little too magical; some maybe would prefer a proof where they could say, "oh, I could have come up with that!".)

4. One thing that AI models seem to be a little weak at is in having "good taste"; in fact, this is also one of the reasons that learning from an AI tutor won't teach you everything important about math. I can totally believe that models could very soon acquire taste, but then there is still the issue that having an AI deciding what is tasteful disempowers humans, which is related to what I wrote about in item 1.

One way that you can see the problem of taste is when you see all the proofs that are posted online on arxiv by people without a lot of training in math research. They have access to a lot of computing power and very powerful models, yet the problems they choose to apply it to end up being like magic trick problems at a carnival (and to be fair, a lot of Erdos's problems were like that, too; but often ones that looked like carnival problems had some hidden motivation that totally escapes the random student plugging it into Astra).

5. I suppose I forgot to say that I'm really excited by all the math advances being made with AI! Quasi-Riemann (or Quasi-GRH), Erdos-Turan, Artin's Conjecture, disproof of Exponential Time Hypothesis, etc. It's just unreal! What a time to be alive!

I will post more, later...

An AI prediction I made 4 years ago, in November 2022 (before ChatGPT was released), for a colleague.

The email I sent was as follows: 

  

  From: Croot, Ernest S <ernest.croot@math.gatech.edu>
  Sent: Sunday, November 13, 2022 9:24 AM 
  To: XXXX, XXXX
  Subject: XXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXXX

  Dear XXXX XXXX,

  Mainly I just think about whether I have enough money to get past the next 10 years (I have thought about 
  retiring early, say in the next 5 years).  After that, I expect things will get a lot cheaper, due to 
  automation, and that people could live off their retirement fairly comfortably, even if they retire early.

  The only concern I think one should have about all these tech developments near term (next 2 to 5 years) 
  is how the administration reacts and how students react.  The last time the MOOC stuff hit, they got very 
  worried, but didn't really do a whole lot (except to convene some school-wide committees to look into it 
  and make recommendations).  This time, they'll probably worry that MIT or Stanford or some other big-name 
  school will offer online courses enhanced with bots -- students watch lecture videos, then the bots released 
  by MIT (in partnership with Microsoft, say) answer all their questions (serving as TA), grade their homeworks, 
  and even their exams and projects.  The worry would be that many more students might take courses this way 
  through those big schools, and get the MIT imprimatur on their transcript; those students won't be doing the 
  same with Georgia Tech.  That was the worry with MOOCs before.

  There'll probably be more demands on professors to make their in-person classes livelier and more engaging, 
  and teaching will become much more important than it is now.   The people who are good teachers, that the 
  students like, will be the big winners.  People who are only decent (but not top) at research, but are sub-par 
  teachers (perhaps due to language barriers), might have some serious problems.

  Further out, over the next 7 to 15 years, the field of math will transition to something more like the field 
  of "history" (which is more about scholarly work and interpretation).  Being a hot-shot researcher who can 
  come up with deep theories and deep proofs worthy of Annals will no longer have much value, as machines will 
  be able to write thousands and thousands of Annals-worthy proofs every day, and even generate new objects to 
  study, well-chosen definitions, etc.  Most major research prizes like the Fields Medal and the Cole Prize 
  will probably be disbanded, since it won't be possible to tell whether a human or machine did the critical 
  work; and these prizes are currently about awarding humans for their brilliant insights.

  It will still be necessary to have humans in the loop to guide these machine creations, and to read and 
  understand them.  Thus, having people who are good scholars will have value to society.  That is what 
  mathematicians will turn into, gradually, over the next 7 to 15 years, as I suggested.


  Best wishes,

  Ernie
I recently described in an email to some colleagues what my reasoning at that time (4 years ago!) was. Here is what I said related to "autoformalization", a component of the overall process of how AI will impact math: 
 

  I sort of imagined autoformalization (e.g. doing it for Fermat's Last Theorem, as we just saw) would come 
  from a different path than Szegedy proposed, however.  I imagined it would proceed like this:  model-makers 
  would create larger and larger models, and find a way to extend the context window length far beyond the 
  limits that existed at the time and get around the "quadratic attention bottleneck" that I knew about then.  
  And then I also thought they would train models to do better and better at in-context learning.  Once it got 
  good enough, they could feed the model a carefully-prepared long prompt spanning thousands of pages, basically 
  teaching a model all the tricks for how to do research math and to program in Lean.  This ultra-long prompt 
  would include not just "facts", but also experiments and examples of how to solve various problems, as well 
  as showing when some particular method failed (so, positive and negative examples, both). In addition, the 
  model could acquire even *more* skills by running many experiments *itself*, all within that ultra-long 
  context window (millions of tokens long).  In very short order it would get to the point where you could feed 
  it Fermat's Last Theorem, and it would go away and write up a perfect auto-formalized version and then verify 
  in Lean or some other proof-verification language / system.

  And, by the way, when models leverage prior context, they don't just have access to the *words* in that 
  context, but the whole KV-cache of forward-pass inferences leading to predictions for next tokens.  It's like 
  not only having access to the text, but also all the "feels" and "experiences" you have as you read it.

That may or may not actually be how it turned out, but at least I had some kind of mechanism in mind how we go from being good at basic arithmetic to being good at proving theorems. The idea sort of treats using in-context learning like using a fuzzy programming language, in this case, plain natural langauge. Natural language is incredibly expressive and compact; however, it is highly context-dependent, which would take massive effort to get AI models to learn to use it like I described.


At the time (4 years ago), I saw a lot of skepticism on the internet about "oh, models just memorize and regurgitate", "they just interpolate", "they're just a simple finite state machine, like in basic linear algebra", and "it's just matrix multiplication, how smart could it be?" None of this impressed me. I knew models were at least capable of running arbitrary programs, at least if you encode them in the right way in the context window, and if the window is large enough to contain the execution traces (the memory stored in LLMs is in the context window; they don't have permanent hidden state memory); all that "interpolation" business seemed not to take algorithms and dynamic processes seriously (when you start iterating the token-generation process, intuitions about models being "curve-fitters" or "regression models" can lead you astray; you start to become governed by "computability" and "complexity", a different ballgame); the "finite state machine" comment is wrong, because the number of states is larger than the number of atoms in the universe (the "states" are implicitly defined; you can't write them all down in a table); and, the "matrix multiplication" one is like saying "the mind is generated by patterns of energy, chemicals, and electrical signals... how complicated can it be?"

Looking further back, back to 2017, I recall seeing some surveys that AI safety people conducted to get some idea how long it would take until AI models could perform certain real-world tasks like "win the Putnam exam". They decided to contact machine learning and AI experts, with qualifications based on whether they published in major conferences like NeurIPS (at the time called NIPS). The median time this team of experts gave for when AI models could ace the Putnam was about 2050; and most thought it would be at least until the mid-2030s. I remeber thinking at the time, "Really? Lol!, you really think it will take that long?" I thought it would take until the late 2020's, like maybe 2028 or so. I was off by just a few years.

In this case (Putnam), I think the conservative estimates were mostly due to people thinking that doing high-level math is a mysterious, creative process that will require deep insight into how human thinking works or something. My thought at the time was that doing math is an algorithmic process that can be encoded into a machine, given enough effort, and that it's not really that mysterious. It's just that people run slightly different algorithms and heuristics, have different memory capacities, different rates of processing, and better or worse probability estimates that bias them towards taking one path versus another in solving a problem. These algorithms and heuristics can be found and coded-up into a machine; or can be learned by machine learning models, given enough examples.

Nowadays, in late 2026, many people have accepted that AI models can prove hard theorems. However, many still think there is going to be a long time until they can invent "theories" and do the "truly original" type of math attributed to people like Grothendieck. My reaction to that is that, again, it won't take as long as people think. Models already can come up with theories, to some degree. Doing it at a very high level is just a matter of having the right algorithms, heristics, and biases, just like before. Except this time, it will happen at a "meta-level". That is, at the level of thinking about thinking.

Postscript: Reflecting on some of this, I remembered another reason people were skeptical about the future of Large Language Models (LLMS), specifically that they couldn't learn "causality". The claim was that, yes, maybe they are good at learning correlations, but that is distinct from learning causation. This argument also didn't impress me. First or all, internet text contains direct mentions of causation, like that eating too much causes you to gain weight. And second, it also contains the natural langauge programs (for lack of a better term) for how to perform tests and interventions to discover more cause-and-effect relations. If a model absorbs these "programs", then it can run tests of its own, just like humans do to ferret out how things work. Another option is to just write these natural language programs out as prompts, which one could think of as a "skill" the model could call on. In either case, LLMs are operating at a higher level than causal inference engines of yore. They can operate at the level that learns the causal inference engine, and then execute it.

Post-Postscript: I guess another criticism that got some buzz was the "stochastic parrots" concept. Basically, it says models only learn text patterns, not the deeper semantics. It's all couched in some language like "surface forms" so that non-linguists attempting to refute it can be criticized for not using the proper protocols of academic linguistics. However, this is self-defeating, because nobody (except linguists) is going to pay it any attention if you have to resort to using phrases like "surface forms". Even so, I am not very impressed by the stochastic parrots argument. If one attempts to apply it to all the possbile text on the internet, which could include the execution traces of an advanced AI somebody designed, then any system trained on it should be able to emulate that AI program -- so you would get a copy of the AI as a consequence of just trying to predict text patterns. Perhaps some priors would be needed for this, but they would probably be very weak; and existing LLMs may already have them.
