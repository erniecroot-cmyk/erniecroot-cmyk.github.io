Research Statement

I decided to write a kind of rambling, non-traditional research statement, instead of a kind of formal and polished one that people often post online. I also wanted to make it short, and just describe the core themes that motivate me.

Where to begin? I think I'll start discussing an experience I had in college. I think I was maybe a sophomore or junior. At the time I was incredibly motivated to do math, and was one of those students my professors probably were a little bit annoyed by. I would read about some problem I had read in one of the undergraduate math journals like MAA Monthly, and would go away and solve it, and slide the solution under their doors. An hour later I would find an error, then would go back to their office and give them the correction. Then maybe there was a third trip to their office. They probably thought I needed some therapy to "calm down!" I would spend hours and hours, way into the evening sometimes working on math puzzles. (A few years prior I was equally obsessed about computer programming. I was one of those computer nerds that wrote endless amounts of code in high school.)

One day, however, I encountered a problem that I couldn't solve. It was very easy to state, but one that I had a hard time believing had an easy solution. It was the following: suppose you color each positive integer one of the r colors (in other words, you partition the positive integers into at most r sets). Then, there exist 3 positive integers x, y, z, all of the same color, such that x + y = z.

After being unable to solve it, I went to the library to read a book about Ramsey Theory, and saw Schur's proof. I was absolutely floored by it! Reading that really made my day! In fact, I was elated for at least a week or two more after that. The idea that no matter how you try to avoid it there can be patterns lurking in the chaos of random (or adversarial) choices, and furthermore that you could actually prove it must exist, was intoxicating.

Some time after that, perhaps when I was a senior, I had another profound experience like that. I was reading about integer factoring algorithms, where you given an integer N that is a product of primes, and you want to determine its prime factorization (in the case when N is composite). I quickly saw how one can do this using at most sqrt(N) steps, but was astonished to read that it could be done using only exp(c sqrt(log N loglog N)) steps. I thought to myself, "how in the world could that be possible!?" And then I read about the "Quadratic Sieve" of Carl Pomerance, and I was absolutely astounded by it.

I was very lucky at the time, because Andrew Granville (who worked at UGA at the time with Carl) gave a talk near my school, and I think he spoke on exactly that topic. I attended, and probably annoyingly answered every question he asked. (I used to do this a lot when people gave talks, but don't do it any more.) Shortly thereafter I became a grad student at UGA and worked with Andrew (and Carl).

My ph.d. thesis was on "unit fractions", and it could be interpreted as a blending of these two "profound experiences" -- Ramsey theory / combinatorics and then also number theory, mostly of a computational, analytic flavor. It is actually kind of strange mix. People who study Ramsey theory and extremal combinatorics very deeply don't usually mess much with number theory; and people who work in number theory tend to avoid combinatorics. I suppose if I looked deeply enough, I would find that these interests of mine go even further back, back to when I was in high school of even elementary school. For example, since I grew up doing a lot of programming (from a very early age), I had a very strong urge to think in computational and algorithmic terms.

And now that I'm an old man -- or middle-aged man, at least -- I don't have quite the same zany passion as I did when I was young; but some of the old me does still remain. Of late, I have become fascinated with how algebraic structures are often the optimal / extremal configuration in certain problems. For example, if you consider the largest or near-largest Sidon subsets of the integers mod N, say, then they turn out to be certain algebraic sets.

It's a bit weird, isn't it? I mean, you have a problem that mentions no algebra at all, that seems too messy to involve any; yet on the other hand, algebra seems to give you the optimal configuration. I feel like there is a deeper reason for this than anyone has yet uncovered...
